A five-question quiz to end a lesson: running an exit ticket on student phones

Ahmed Moaz · 16 October 2026

An exit ticket is a short check at the end of a lesson: a few questions every student answers before they leave. It tells you, in a couple of minutes, who understood today's idea and who did not. Done on paper it means a stack to mark. Done as a quick quiz, you can see the result before the bell.

Here is a plan for a five-question version, and an honest note on where a ready-made game fits.

Why five questions

Five is enough to cover the main idea of a lesson from more than one angle, and short enough that students finish in two or three minutes. Fewer than three tells you little. More than eight turns a check into a test, and students treat it like one.

Write the five in this order

  1. Recall. One question on a fact or term from today. Everyone who listened should get it.
  2. Recognise. The idea in a new example. "Which of these is an example of…?"
  3. Apply. A small problem using today's method.
  4. Spot the mistake. Show a worked answer with one error and ask what went wrong.
  5. Stretch. One question that goes a step past the lesson, for the students who found it easy.

Make every question multiple choice with four answers. It is quick to answer on a phone and quick to read at a glance.

Write wrong answers that teach you something

The three wrong options are where an exit ticket earns its keep. Each should be a mistake a real student would make: the sign error, the reversed definition, the step that was skipped. When a third of the class picks the same wrong answer, you know exactly what to re-teach tomorrow.

Avoid joke options. They are easy to rule out, so a question with one joke option is really a three-choice question.

Running it in the last ten minutes

  • Minute 0 to 1: students open the quiz on their phones or tablets. Use a tool that needs no app or account, or you will lose five minutes to passwords.
  • Minute 1 to 4: everyone answers all five questions. Everyone answers every question, so no student can sit back while a quick classmate answers for the group.
  • Minute 4 to 8: go through the answers together, spending the time on the question most students missed.
  • Minute 8 to 10: note which students to check in with next lesson.

Many free quiz tools let you type in your own questions for this. Use one of those for the exit ticket itself, because the point is to check today's lesson.

When you have no time to write questions

Some days the lesson overruns and there are ten minutes left with nothing prepared. When there is no time to write your own questions, a ready-made general-knowledge round such as Trivia Tavern on Boardit, which I build, can fill the last ten minutes instead. It is multiple choice with four answers, everyone answers every question on their own phone, and the teacher can pick categories such as geography or science.

To be clear, it does not take your own questions. It is a filler, not an exit ticket, and it checks general knowledge rather than today's work. On a day when the plan falls apart, that is still better than ten minutes of packing up early.

Keep it low stakes

An exit ticket is for you, not for grades. Tell students that, and they answer honestly instead of guessing at what you want. A wrong answer on a Tuesday afternoon is useful information, not a failure.

What I cannot tell you

I have no measurements of how much exit tickets improve results. This is a planning template, and the minute-by-minute timings are a suggestion to adjust for your class.
