A property trading game for six players: seating, pace and keeping everyone in

Ahmed Moaz · 25 October 2026

Six is the largest table Property Rush allows, and it is the hardest to run. Six people mean six sets of deals, a long wait between turns and a board with only 28 things to buy. Property Rush is an independent Monopoly alternative I build, and I have thought a good deal about how to make a six-player property trading game work. Here is the advice.

What is different at a big table?

Three things.

First, scarcity. There are 28 purchasable properties: 22 cities, 4 airports and 2 utilities. Among six players, that is under five each. Most players will not complete a country without a trade.

Second, time. You wait through five other turns between your own. Even if each takes a minute, that is five minutes of watching.

Third, endings. A note in the game's engine records an earlier simulation: inside a fixed budget of moves, 88% of two-player games reached a winner, against only 12% at eight players. At six, the last-player-standing ending is already slow. More players means more capital chasing the same properties, so bankruptcies get rarer.

What should the host switch on?

For six, I would suggest:

  • A smaller starting purse, so money runs short sooner. Without it, the evening may not end.
  • Auctions on, so cities change hands fast.
  • Rent doubling for a complete country on, so that trades are worth the effort.
  • Mortgages on, so nobody is knocked out early.

A lower starting cash can also tighten the game, but I would leave it at 1,500 for a first try.

How do you keep people engaged?

The risk is boredom. Some ideas:

  1. Let spectators join auctions. Bidding is a form of play.
  2. Encourage trades during other players' turns. They do not need to wait.
  3. Give each player a role: banker, timekeeper, rule-checker.
  4. Keep chat going. A property game is half conversation.

What is a good seating plan?

Mix experienced and new players. Put the quiet person between two chatty ones. Seat rivals next to each other, because they will trade or fight, and keep the group's attention on the board.

How long will it take?

The game lists about 45 minutes, which is realistic for two to four players. At six, plan for an hour and a half or more, a little less with a smaller starting purse. A round at six players might take five to eight minutes once trading starts.

What if someone goes bankrupt early?

They are out, but the rules are gentle: a rent you cannot cover becomes a debt that you clear by mortgaging, selling or trading, and you leave only by declaring bankruptcy or walking away. If a player leaves early, give them a new role, such as commentator or auctioneer's assistant. It keeps them in the evening.

What happens to trading at a big table?

It becomes a market. With six players, several deals can be live at once, and prices become public. If one player offers 250 for a missing city and another offers 300, everyone sees it. That helps beginners, who can learn what things are worth by watching, and it helps sellers, who can ask for more. It also means that a player holding the last city of a set is in a strong position, and should not rush to sell.

One rule of thumb helps new players at a large table: make your offers out loud and keep them short. "Venice for 280, anyone?" is faster than a private negotiation, and it invites a counter-offer.

Is six worth it?

For a party, yes. The game becomes a social event with deals across the table. For a serious match, four is better. Choose by mood.

A parting suggestion

Run the first game with a limit of fifteen rounds. If everyone enjoys it, extend it next time. A short game leaves people wanting more, which is the ideal state for a large group.
