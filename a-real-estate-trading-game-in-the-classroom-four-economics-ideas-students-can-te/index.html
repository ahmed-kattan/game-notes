A real estate trading game in the classroom: four economics ideas students can test

Ahmed Moaz · 19 October 2026

Teachers of economics often say that the surest way to explain scarcity, return on investment and rent is to let students feel them. A real estate trading game, which is a kind of property trading game, does that in a way a textbook cannot. Property Rush, an independent Monopoly alternative I build, runs in a browser, and its economy is simple enough to analyse in a lesson. Here are four ideas to build a class around, each with numbers to check.

Idea one: return on investment

Ask students to estimate how long a city takes to repay its price. Most guess ten or twenty turns. Then give the figures from the model: on bare land, a city needs between 293 and 1,434 opponent stops, which means never within a game. With three houses it needs 26 to 74. The lesson is that capital without improvement earns almost nothing, and improvement multiplies it.

Students can calculate their own paybacks by dividing a city's cost by its expected rent. They need the rent table and an estimate of how often a token lands on the city, about one stop in forty.

Idea two: location and demand

Not all land is equal. In the model, Prison takes 12.0% of all stops, and the busiest city, Osaka, 3.26%, while an even spread would be 2.5%. Ask students why some locations earn more and what that says about real cities. Then ask which would matter more to a landlord, price or traffic.

Idea three: economies of scale

A single city pays little. A whole country doubles the rent on bare land and allows buildings. Ask why a set is worth more than its parts, and how that compares to a chain of shops or a block of flats owned by one company.

Idea four: land and rent

The first property game, The Landlord's Game, was designed to show a thinker's argument that rent enriches the owners of land at the expense of those who pay it. In a class game, watch what happens: after the first hour, one or two players own the busy countries and the rest pay. Ask students whether that feels fair and what a rule could do about it. The historical game had a second rule set aimed at exactly that.

How do you run the lesson?

Plan fifty minutes. Spend ten on the model, thirty playing in groups of three or four with the smallest starting purse, stopping when the time is up, and ten on discussion. A shortened game is enough to see all four ideas. Ask each group to keep a log of what they bought, what it cost and what it earned.

What are the follow-up questions?

  • Which purchase paid back fastest, and why?
  • Did anyone regret a purchase?
  • How did the auction change prices?
  • What would you change in the rules to make the game fairer?

Where can you run it?

A browser table makes classroom play simple. At Board It each group opens a table for two to six players, plays a property game online, and needs no download. Students can continue with friends at home, and the data they gather makes a good homework task.

How do you assess it?

Ask each student to hand in a half page: one decision they made, the numbers behind it and what they would do differently. Marking is quick, and the writing shows who understood return on investment and who simply followed the dice. For older students, set a short task: given the rent table and the stop rate, calculate the payback of a hotel on Germany. The answer, around 33 opponent stops for the whole country, is a good check.

You can also pair the lesson with a short reading on the history of the game, and ask whether its inventor would approve of the way the class played. The answers are often thoughtful.

A caution

The game simplifies a lot. Real markets involve credit, regulation and risk of a kind a board cannot show. Present it as a model, not a mirror.
