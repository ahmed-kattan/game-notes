An empty seat at the table: how bots fill it in a Monopoly alternative

Ahmed Moaz · 28 October 2026

A property game with three people at a table built for four feels wrong. The money moves faster, the same two rivals trade back and forth, and the third player has no one to bargain with. If you are searching for a Monopoly alternative that works when a friend cancels, the way it handles an empty chair is worth checking. In Property Rush, the free property trading game I build, the host may seat a bot there instead.

What does a bot actually do?

It plays the game. In my play tests, bots rolled, bought, built, bid at auctions and offered deals without help. Their names all begin with "Bot", so you always know which seat is which.

It does not talk. A bot cannot argue, flatter or bluff. It makes a proposal, and you can accept it or turn it down. That is a real limit, and it means a table of bots is a quieter place than a table of friends.

Are its trade offers fair to you?

I decided early that they should be. A bot puts a deal to a person only when it judges your gain to be no smaller than its own. It weighs cash at face value, a city at its printed price, and a whole country at a premium, because completing a set is what makes a city worth having.

I wrote that rule after an early bot proposed a swap that favoured itself too much. Now an offer should never be a trick. You still have to judge it with your own head. The bot's measure is a model, and a model can be wrong about what a particular city means to you.

Can a bot beat a person?

I tested one version against a simple opponent. Across 300 two-player games played by programs, an earlier bot won 67% of the games that reached a result, against a player that only buys and builds and never trades. An even match would be 50%.

Treat that carefully. It was one opponent, played by a program, on the rules as they stood on 16 October 2026. It does not show how a bot fares against someone who negotiates well. I would say the bot is a competent filler, not a champion.

What happens when a person leaves?

Bots do a second job. They stand in for people who go quiet. A turn gets a minute, and after that a player draws on a personal reserve of three minutes. If the reserve runs dry, a bot completes that turn cautiously: it will not buy, build or bid, and it offers no deals. After two missed turns running, it plays the seat completely until the person returns.

So a lost connection never ends the game for the others. And nobody is thrown out.

How do you add one?

The host sets it up in the lobby, ahead of the first roll. Tables hold two to six players. You can run a game with one person and one bot, or fill every free chair. I run Property Rush on Boardit, where a guest joins by choosing a display name.

Bots exist in two other games on the site, Ludo and Snakes and Ladders. Trivia, Who's the Spy and Draw and Guess do not have them, since a bot would need language to take part.

When would I choose a bot?

I would choose one in three cases. When a friend cancels at the last minute. When you want to practise a new rule before inviting people. And when you are alone and want to learn how rents work.

I would not use one to replace a friend who is usually there. A person at the table brings talk and surprise, and a bot does not.

Property Rush is an independent Monopoly alternative, and it is meant for online play with friends, with bots only as a spare.
