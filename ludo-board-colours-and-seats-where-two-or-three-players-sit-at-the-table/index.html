Ludo board colours and seats: where two or three players sit at the table

Ahmed Moaz · 6 November 2026

A ludo board has four corners and four colours, yet plenty of games have fewer than four people. Two friends on a call, three cousins and a spare evening: what happens to the corners nobody takes? Here is how my Boardit table seats a small group, how colours are chosen, and what the empty corners mean when you play ludo online.

Which colours can you choose?

There are nine. The first four are the colours most people picture when they hear the word ludo: red, green, yellow and blue. The other five are spares: terracotta, sage, pink, plum and teal.

In ludo the colour you pick for your seat is also the colour of your corner of the board, so the choice carries through the whole game. Nine colours for at most four seats means the last person to choose still has a choice.

How does the table pick a colour for you?

If you have not chosen, the table looks at the colour saved in your profile. It offers the nearest free colour if one is close enough to read as the same shade, and a random free colour if not. You can still pick another.

Bots follow a polite rule. They wear clearly different colours, and they give a colour up when a person picks it. So you never have to fight a bot for yellow.

Where do two players sit?

In opposite corners. The board has four corners, numbered clockwise from the top left, and two players take the first and the third. The point is fairness. Seated side by side, one player would start a quarter-turn of the track behind the other, so opposite corners keep the starts equal.

The doors, the squares where a six frees a piece, sit 13 squares apart around the 52-square track. Opposite doors are therefore 26 squares apart in both directions. Each player's nearest rival door is exactly half the loop behind them.

Where do three players sit?

Three take three corners in a row and the fourth stays empty. Between neighbouring colours the gap is 13 squares. Between the last colour and the first, across the empty corner, it is 26.

By the arithmetic of the doors, the player who comes first in turn order sits just clockwise of the empty corner. For that player, the nearest rival door behind them is 26 squares away, not 13. I would not call that an advantage, since I have not measured it. It is a feature of the geometry that a three-player game has and a four-player game does not.

Does the empty corner matter?

Less than you might expect. Safe squares belong to the square and not to a player, so an empty corner's front door is still safe for everyone who passes over it. Its home column simply goes unused, and nobody ever walks into it.

The result is a track with the same 52 squares and the same eight safe ones, only fewer pieces on it. I would expect fewer captures with two or three players than with four, simply because fewer pieces share the loop.

Who goes first, and does the board always look the same?

Turn order follows the seats in the lobby. The whole arrangement is then turned by a random quarter-turn. A two-player game keeps its two colours opposite, and a three-player game keeps its trio together, but the corners vary from one game to the next. The layout is not the same in every game.

What should you agree before choosing?

Settle colours the way you would settle seats: out loud, early, and with a rule for who yields. Someone who likes red will probably ask for it again. Offer it and take a spare, since the spares work just as well on the board.

Then open a table at Boardit's Ludo and watch where each person is placed. For ludo with friends, two or three players can sit down in a minute.

A board that fits the table

A small table does not play a lesser game. It plays the same ludo rules on the same track, with room to breathe. Pick a colour, take your corner and enjoy the extra space.
