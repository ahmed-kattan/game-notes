Ludo rules and the exact finish: why the last steps cost about five throws

Ahmed Moaz · 21 October 2026

Of all the ludo rules, the exact finish is the one that makes people sigh. You have worked your piece round the entire board, and now you need precisely the right number to bring it in. Throw anything bigger and it cannot move. I wanted to know how much that single rule costs, and the answer is neat.

What does the rule say?

To land in the centre, a piece must use the exact number of steps it has left. If it has three to go, a three brings it home. A four, five or six does nothing for that piece. It can still move another piece, if you have one on the board.

How much does it cost?

On my board a piece needs 56 steps from its entry square to home. Without the rule, so that any throw big enough counts, the journey would take about 16 throws, which is 56 divided by the average throw of 3.5. With the rule, a lone piece needs 20.76 throws. The difference is about 4.8 throws, nearly a quarter of the whole journey.

Why is the last stretch so slow?

Once a piece is within six squares of home, the wait follows a very regular pattern. Only one number gets it home, so each throw has a one in six chance. That means an average of six throws, whether the piece is one square away or six.

The pleasing thing is that the distance does not matter. A piece four squares away does not have an easier job than one that is five away. Both need one particular number.

What does this mean in play?

  • Do not hurry a piece to the very last square if you have other useful moves.
  • A piece on its way to the centre can use small throws to creep closer, but remember that you may be giving up flexibility.
  • Keep a spare piece on the board, so that a throw that does not fit has somewhere to go.

For a player with four pieces, the exact finish is a source of tension, not a trap. The skill is in arranging your pieces so that you waste few throws.

Is the rule worth keeping?

Many families keep it, because it gives the end of the game a shape. Without it, the winner is simply the one who gets there first, and everyone races to the last square. With it, the final moves become a small drama in which luck and planning fight each other.

Some families relax it, for example by letting a piece finish on any throw that is at least what it needs. It speeds up the game by about five throws per piece. If you are playing with young children, that may be a kindness.

How do you set the rule at a table?

Say it aloud before you start. "To come home you need the exact number. If you throw more, that piece stays." On a free table for ludo online, such as the one at Board It, the exact finish is built in. It is a multiplayer game for two to four players, and you can play ludo with friends on separate devices with the rules checked for you.

A test you can run yourself

Play ten turns with one piece, ignoring everything else, and count how many throws it takes to get home. You will see journeys of 12 and journeys of 35. The average will settle around 20 after a few hundred games, which is a lot of Ludo, but the spread alone teaches the point.

The honest limit

With four pieces, the cost of the exact finish is partly hidden, because other pieces use the throws that one piece cannot. So the five-throw figure is easiest to read as the weight of the rule on a single journey.
