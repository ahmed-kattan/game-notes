Real estate trading game rent ladders explained, with the numbers from Abu Dhabi to Toronto

Ahmed Moaz · 30 October 2026

Every real estate trading game has a rent ladder, even if it never calls it that. It is the list of amounts a city can charge: one for the bare city, then more for each step of building. Learn the ladder and you can tell, before you spend a coin, where your money works hardest. I wrote the ladders for my property trading game, Property Rush, so I can show you every rung with the real numbers.

What are the six rungs?

A city in Property Rush has six rents. The first is the bare city. The next four apply with one, two, three and four houses. The sixth is a hotel. Here are four cities, from the cheapest to the dearest I will use.

A house costs 50 in the UAE and Egypt and 200 in Canada. The hotel is priced like a fifth house.

Why does a bare city pay so little?

Look at the bare column. Abu Dhabi charges 2 on a price of 60, which is about three percent. Toronto charges 50 on 400, about twelve and a half percent. Nobody gets rich from bare cities. The ladder is built so that rent stays small until you invest.

That is the point of the design. A rent that rises only when you build turns spending into a plan, because you must decide which city deserves your next house.

Which step pays the most?

Subtract each rung from the next and you see how much extra rent one house adds each time someone lands. On Giza the steps are 24, 60, 180, 130 and 150. On Cairo they are 32, 60, 200, 150 and 150. On Toronto they are 150, 400, 800, 300 and 300.

On those three ladders the third house is the biggest jump. A third house on Giza adds 180 for a cost of 50, which is 3.6 for every unit spent. On Toronto it adds 800 for 200, which is 4.0. Compare the first house: on Giza it adds 24 for 50, less than one for one. On Abu Dhabi, the third house adds only 60, so a unit spent returns 1.2.

So the cheap cities do not reward building the way the dear ones do. Abu Dhabi's rungs are smaller than Giza's, and both use the same 50 house.

How much does it cost to climb?

You need money for every rung. Taking Giza to a hotel costs five builds of 50, which is 250. Doing the same on all three Egyptian cities costs 750. On Toronto one hotel costs 1,000, and a hotel on both Canadian cities costs 2,000.

Compare that with the starting purse. The standard purse is $1,500, and four other amounts are offered. On paper, $1,500 covers all three Egyptian cities (330) and hotels on each (750). That is 1,080 gone before you have bought anything else, so the ladder rewards patience.

Does owning the whole country change the ladder?

Boardit runs Property Rush as a free browser game, so you can set up a game and read the ladders on the board yourself.

Hold a whole country and the bare rent doubles before any building. Giza goes from 6 to 12, and Cairo from 8 to 16. Toronto goes from 50 to 100 once you also hold Vancouver. This bonus touches only the bottom rung, and the host can switch it off.

Houses must also be added level across a country, and the bank holds thirty-two houses and twelve hotels. A rival who builds first can leave you waiting for a hotel.

What I take from the numbers

The ladders were authored by me, one at a time, for an independent Monopoly alternative, and they show three habits worth copying in any real estate trading game. Keep the bare rent low, so owning is cheap. Put the biggest jump near the third rung, so building has a payoff worth planning for. Keep the last two rungs close, so a hotel is a strong finish, not a rescue.

When you play online with friends, watch where your table builds first. On these numbers, the third house is where a country starts to hurt rivals. Count it in your own plan before the dice decide it for you.

Where the numbers stop

These figures describe a landing, not a game. They do not say how often anyone lands on a given city. The extra rent per unit spent is a measure of a build, not a promise of profit. Use them to compare options, then watch your table.
