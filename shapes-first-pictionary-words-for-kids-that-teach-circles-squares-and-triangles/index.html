Shapes first: pictionary words for kids that teach circles, squares and triangles

Ahmed Moaz · 29 October 2026

Every picture is made of a few simple shapes, and a child who sees that can draw almost anything. This idea turns a word list into a lesson without anyone noticing. These pictionary words for kids are grouped by the shape they are built from: circles, squares, triangles, rectangles, ovals and combinations. There are 54 words, written for this page. Pictionary is the name of a game owned by another company; I have no tie to it, and I use the word only because it is the phrase people search for.

Why start a drawing game with shapes?

Because a blank page is the hard part. A child who is told to "draw a clock" does not know where to put the pencil. A child who is told that a clock is a circle with two lines inside starts straight away.

Shapes also give the table a second way to guess. When a drawer starts with a triangle, the other players can shout "roof", "tent" or "sail", and the group is playing a game of narrowing down. That is the heart of any drawing game.

How do you teach the shape trick in one minute?

Show it with one example. Draw a circle, then add two smaller circles on top, and ask what it is. Add a face and it becomes a bear. Draw a square, put a triangle on top, and it is a house.

Then say the rule aloud: "Find the biggest shape first." It is the only rule this game needs. It is my own rule of thumb, not a teaching standard; it simply makes the first mark easy.

Can older players use the same list online?

Children under 13 are not who Boardit is for, so the children should stay on paper. A teenager or adult who wants the same list on a screen can open a Draw and Guess table at Boardit, a free site I make, and send the link to up to eleven others.

Players give a name and nothing else. The host can supply a private list of words, hidden from the other players, and each drawer is shown five options to choose from, which makes the shape groups easy to mix. A shape-first list does well in a drawing game online with friends, because the guessers learn to read the first strokes quickly.

Every drawing is kept in a gallery when the game ends. For a list like this the gallery is a good way to see whether the circles looked like circles, and whether the squares stayed square.

What should the next list teach?

Once the shapes are easy, change one thing at a time. Add a second shape to each word, or remove the outline and allow only lines. Try a round where the drawer may use only three strokes, which is harder than it sounds and makes the table very quiet. If the shapes still feel easy, move on to the words for ages 8 to 11 and keep the shape rule as a warm-up.

Over several evenings, keep the words that were guessed fastest in the pile you want to repeat, and send the rest back to the middle of the list. A good children's list is never finished, and the clearest sign that it works is a child asking to play it again.
