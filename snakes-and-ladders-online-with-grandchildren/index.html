Snakes and Ladders online with grandchildren

Ahmed Moaz · 14 October 2026

You can play Snakes and Ladders online with grandchildren in about fifteen minutes of game time and five of setup. One host sets up a table in a browser, everyone else types a name, and a video call in another app carries the chatter. In a Snakes and Ladders game every move is forced, so a grandparent and a teenager start exactly level. It is one of the easier online board games for a family video call.

One honest limit comes first. The site this guide uses asks players to be at least 13, so this is for teenage and adult grandchildren. Younger ones are better served by the board on your own shelf.

Set it up in four steps

  1. Start the video call first. Use whatever your family already uses. Put the call on one device and the game on another if you can, say a phone for the call and a tablet or laptop for the board.
  2. Someone hosts. Whoever is most comfortable with the screen should be the host. The table gives out a short code, and the host sends it in the call's chat or reads it aloud.
  3. Everyone types a name. Guests need no email address or password.
  4. The host starts the game when every seat is filled. A table seats two to eight, so a whole branch of the family can join one multiplayer board.

On a first visit there is a short "How it works" guide, and at every table a hint says what to do now. That is the part a grandparent who is not sure what to do next can lean on.

How long does a Snakes and Ladders game take?

Roughly ten minutes for two players, fifteen for four and twenty-three for eight. Those come from 52, 75 and 117 throws a game in simulated play, at about twelve seconds a throw. A call with chatting in it will run longer, so book twenty minutes for four.

A 45-minute call, minute by minute

  • 0:00 Everyone joins the call. Say hello before anyone touches the game.
  • 0:05 The host sets up the table and sends the code. Others type a name.
  • 0:10 The first game starts. Four players, about fifteen minutes.
  • 0:25 Break. Whoever won picks what happens next.
  • 0:30 Second game, started from the same table.
  • 0:45 Stop while it is still fun.

Turn order follows the seats in the lobby, so first throw goes to whoever sits first. In a game with no decisions, that is the only thing worth arguing about.

What to expect on the board

Here is how to play Snakes and Ladders over a video call without explaining much. One throw a turn, and a six earns no bonus. Land at the foot of a ladder and you climb, land on a snake's head and you slide. Square 100 must be hit exactly, so a player who needs a three and throws a five stays put. Counters never capture each other, and squares can hold as many as arrive, so nobody gets sent home by a relative.

Ten snakes and nine ladders share the board. When a player's clock runs out, the game throws for them, so a call interrupted by a phone ringing does not stop play. Say it plainly to a nervous player: there is nothing to get wrong.

Five small checks before the call

  • Plug the game device in, so a flat battery does not end the evening.
  • Turn the volume on the game device down. Presses make a sound, and on a shared call it comes through as clicking.
  • Send the table code as a message, not out loud. Characters read aloud over a bad line are how people end up at the wrong table.
  • Zoom the browser in if the numbers are small. The board is a hundred squares and it helps to see them.
  • Agree who explains the first turn. One voice is clearer than three.

Your first call

If you have never done this, run a two-player game first with one grandchild, ten minutes, before the family call. The game itself opens in any browser through Boardit, with no download and no account for guests. Then add seats one call at a time, and keep the video call going the whole time. If a grandchild would rather chat than watch the board, let them. The game is a reason to keep the call open, and the talking is the point.
