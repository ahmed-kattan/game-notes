Teaching geography with a quiz: question types that go beyond capitals

Ahmed Moaz · 15 October 2026

A geography quiz can test more than capitals, and it should, because a capital is one fact about a place and not a way of thinking about it. The five themes of geography give a quick checklist: location, place, human-environment interaction, movement and region. If your trivia questions all sit under one, your students are practising one skill. Here is how to spread a trivia quiz across all five, and what a ready-made question bank covers.

The five themes as a checklist

The five themes were set out in 1984 by the Joint Committee on Geographic Education, formed by two US bodies, the National Council for Geographic Education and the Association of American Geographers. They are a way of organising lessons, and they work as a check on a quiz as well. The table pairs each theme with a multiple-choice question I wrote for this piece.

ThemeWhat the question asksExample (four options)
LocationWhere is it, compared with somewhere else?Which of these cities lies furthest north: Cairo, Nairobi, Lagos, Cape Town? (Cairo)
PlaceWhat is it like there?Which of these countries has no coastline: Bolivia, Peru, Chile, Ecuador? (Bolivia)
Human-environment interactionHow do people shape their surroundings?What is the Dutch word for land reclaimed from the sea? (polder)
MovementHow do goods, people or ideas travel?Which strait links the Mediterranean Sea to the Atlantic Ocean? (Gibraltar)
RegionWhat do places share?Which of these is not in Southeast Asia: Laos, Vietnam, Nepal, Cambodia? (Nepal)

Notice that each example asks for a different kind of thinking: comparing latitudes, spotting a feature, naming a process, tracing a route, sorting places into a region. A wrong answer then hints at which skill is weak, and not only which fact is missing.

What a ready-made bank covers

Students already meet trivia questions outside school, often playing with friends, so a quiz format is familiar. I counted what one bank asks. Trivia Tavern's Geography category holds 400 questions in four topics: Countries and capitals, Flags, Landmarks and World cultures. Each topic holds 100 questions: two fifths easy, two fifths medium and one fifth hard.

By keyword count over the question text, which is my method and not a hand classification:

  • 75 of the 400 mention a capital.
  • In the Countries and capitals topic, 17 questions use the word currency and more name one, such as the euro or the peso. Counting capitals and the word currency alone, about 90 of that topic's 100 questions are about one or the other.
  • 91 of the 100 Flags questions use the word flag or banner. The question files have no picture field, so a flag question describes the flag in words. That tests recall from a description, and it is not recognition by sight.
  • 36 mention a festival, holiday or celebration, all in World cultures.
  • Only about 20 mention a river, sea, ocean, mountain, desert, lake, island, strait, canal, peninsula or volcano; the exact figure, 17 to 23, depends on which spellings I count.

Read that as a map of the bank. It is strong on named places, flags and festivals. It has little on movement and little on how people change the land. Those two themes are the ones a teacher should write by hand.

The question writers define "easy" as a question about 85 to 97 percent of a casual table would get right, so the easy tier works as retrieval practice. The hard tier, at 33 to 50 percent, is where a mixed class stretches. A ten-question set drawn evenly from the three tiers will feel very different from one drawn only from the easy tier, so decide which you want before you pick.

The question bank I audited is the one at playboardit.com/play/trivia-tavern, where Geography is one category in the lobby.

Fill the gaps with pictures and maps

Two gaps are easy to close in a lesson. Because the question files carry no picture, a flag question is a description read aloud. Show the flag on the board afterwards and ask which feature gave it away. For movement and human-environment questions, put a blank map on the board and have students mark the answer before the options appear. Every question in the bank also ships with a reveal line after the answer, so a student who disagrees has a reason to argue with.

A ten-question quiz that covers every theme

Mix the bank with your own questions on a slide:

  1. Two questions from the bank's Landmarks topic (place).
  2. Two from Countries and capitals, one a capital and one a currency (location).
  3. Two of your own on movement, such as straits, trade routes or rivers that cross borders.
  4. Two of your own on human-environment interaction, such as dams, terraces or reclaimed land.
  5. Two on region, using a shared border or a shared language.

Players answer on their own phones. The site is not aimed at children under 13, so this suits older students only. The host cannot add custom questions to the bank, which is why items three, four and five come from you.

Is online trivia worth class time?

In my view it earns a short slot for retrieval practice and none for explanation. Online trivia gives every student a question at once and shows a one-line fact after each answer. It cannot tell you why a student chose Peru over Bolivia. Use the quiz to find weak spots, then spend the lesson on the reasons.
