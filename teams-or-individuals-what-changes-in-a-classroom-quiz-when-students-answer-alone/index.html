Teams or individuals: what changes in a classroom quiz when students answer alone

Ahmed Moaz · 16 October 2026

When students answer a quiz alone, every student's knowledge becomes visible and nobody can hide behind the loudest voice. When they answer in teams, the room gets more talk and less certainty about who knew what. Neither is better. Which one to pick depends on what you want the trivia quiz to tell you.

What changes when everyone answers alone

Answering alone changes four things at once.

You see each student's answer, not the team's. A team of four hands in one answer, and it may come from one person while three nod. Alone, you get four answers.

Quiet students get a turn. In a team the confident voice often wins the argument. Alone, a student who knows the answer can give it without winning a debate first.

Pressure goes up. In a quiz scored on the spot, a wrong answer is yours, with nobody to share it. That makes some students careful and some anxious.

Time per question drops. There is no discussion to wait for, so a round of ten trivia questions goes by quickly, whether you run it as online trivia or on paper.

What the research says about effort in groups

Psychologists call it social loafing: people put in less effort when their own contribution cannot be picked out. In a 1979 experiment by Latané, Williams and Harkins, blindfolded students wearing headphones shouted as loudly as they could. When they believed one other person was shouting too, they shouted at 82 percent of their solo intensity. With five others, it fell to 74 percent. The write-up on Wikipedia adds that making individual input identifiable is one of the ways to reduce loafing.

That is the case for individual answers. It is a study of shouting adults, though, not of students answering quiz questions, so treat it as a reason to look, not as proof.

How Trivia Tavern scores it

In that game every player answers every question themselves. Each question is multiple choice, with four options and one answer, and there are no lifelines and no passing. The default clock is 20 seconds. A right answer keeps its full points if it is instant and loses at most a quarter if it lands at the very end of the timer. A wrong answer scores nothing. Standings are visible throughout.

There is no team mode. If you want teams, you build them around the game: players keep their own scores, and you add them up yourself.

A fair way to add them is to make the weakest scorer count double. Here are made-up scores for a team of three after one round: 900, 600 and 200.

  • Plain sum: 900 + 600 + 200 = 1,700.
  • Weakest counts double: 900 + 600 + (2 × 200) = 1,900.

Under the plain sum, 100 extra points from the strongest student add 100 to the team. Under the double rule, 100 extra points from the weakest add 200. The team now has a reason to help the student who is struggling.

A word on who this is for

Boardit is meant for ages 13 and over, and nothing here is a claim about what suits a school. The same ideas apply to any group quiz, including a trivia night with friends or an online quiz with friends on a video call.

A rule to pick with

Use individual answers when you need to know what each person can do, or when your quietest students need an equal turn. Use teams when the point is the talk and not the result. Use both, with the double rule above, when you want each student's own answer to count and the team to share a result.
