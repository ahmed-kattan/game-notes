Ten-minute team games to bookend a sprint retrospective, not replace it

Ahmed Moaz · 17 October 2026

A game is not a retrospective. The Scrum Guide says the retrospective exists "to plan ways to increase quality and effectiveness", and a quiz does none of that.

But ten-minute team games either side of a retro can do two useful things. Before, they work as an agile icebreaker and get people talking. After, they end a sprint on something lighter than a list of action items. I build a small site of free online games, and two of these multiplayer browser games can be squeezed into ten minutes. Here are the honest numbers.

How long should a sprint retrospective be, and where does a game fit?

The Scrum Guide caps the retrospective at three hours for a one-month sprint, and shorter sprints usually get less. That time belongs to the retro. So the game goes in the five or ten minutes while people are still joining the call, or in the slot after the last action item is written down.

Pick the game by the clock, not by the fun. A game that overruns turns a warm-up into another meeting.

Games to play before a retrospective: one quiz round

A quick online quiz for a team meeting suits the start because everyone answers simultaneously, each on a personal phone, so nobody waits for a turn. Trivia Tavern's default answer clock is 20 seconds, and a match can be cut to a single round of 10 questions. I would budget about 6 minutes for that round. Each question holds the screen for at most about 33 seconds, and it closes early once everybody has answered.

A one-round match has no doubled final round, which suits a warm-up: nobody's comeback depends on it. One room seats eight. A bigger team splits into two rooms and compares scores.

After the retro: a drawing game for a remote team

A drawing game suits the end. Guesses are typed in the game's chat, so it also works for anyone who has talked enough for one day. Time is the catch. At the shortest settings, two rounds and a 40-second clock, each turn takes at most 58 seconds. Everyone draws once a round, so the length grows with the team:

  • 4 people: up to 7.7 minutes
  • 5 people: up to 9.7 minutes
  • 8 people: up to 15.5 minutes
  • 10 people: up to 19.3 minutes

For a ten-minute slot, a drawing game fits four or five people. A larger team should take the quiz instead, or split into rooms.

All six of my games run in a browser on Boardit.

Fitting the numbers to a scrum team

The Scrum Guide puts a team at typically 10 or fewer people. For a team of ten, the quiz needs two rooms, and the drawing game needs nearly twenty minutes. That is too long for a bookend. For a team of five, either game fits.

These are ceilings, not stopwatch times. They assume every question and every turn runs to its limit, so a real game usually finishes sooner. I have not clocked how long a team needs to get seated, so leave a little slack for joining.

Keep it optional

I would say it plainly. A game at work only feels like a break if skipping it costs nothing. Say out loud that it's optional, start on time and stop on time.

Optional cuts both ways. Someone who would rather not play can still follow along: since 2 October 2026 a host can let up to 20 people watch a table without taking a seat.

What ten-minute team games won't do

These are online games for remote teams to play, not tools: neither measures anything about the team. There is no facilitator mode, no attendance tracking and no reporting. I have not run either game with a scrum team, so the timings above come from the games' clocks, not from a sprint.
