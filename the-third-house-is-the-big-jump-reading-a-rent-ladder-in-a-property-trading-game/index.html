The third house is the big jump: reading a rent ladder in a property trading game

Ahmed Moaz · 4 November 2026

Every city in a property trading game has a rent ladder: the rent on bare land, then with one house, two, three, four, and finally a hotel. Most players know that more is better. Few ask which step is worth the most. I worked it out for Property Rush, an independent Monopoly alternative I build, and one step stands out clearly.

What does a rent ladder look like?

Take Osaka, in the Japan group. Its rents are 20 on bare land, 100 with one house, 300 with two, 750 with three, 925 with four, and 1,100 with a hotel. A house there costs 150.

What does each house add?

Subtract each step from the one before and divide by the cost of the house. For Osaka the extra rent bought by each 150 is:

  • First house: +80 rent, 0.53 per coin spent
  • Second house: +200, 1.33 per coin
  • Third house: +450, 3.00 per coin
  • Fourth house: +175, 1.17 per coin
  • Hotel: +175, 1.17 per coin

The third house gives three times the return of the first, and more than double the fourth. It is the big jump.

Is that true everywhere?

The pattern holds on the dear side of the board. For Toronto, with houses at 200, the returns are 0.75, 2.00, 4.00, 1.50 and 1.50. For Paris, with houses at 100: 0.64, 1.40, 3.80, 2.00 and 2.00. In each case the third house is the strongest purchase on the ladder, and the first is the weakest.

The cheap end behaves differently. For Abu Dhabi, with houses at 50, the returns are 0.16, 0.40, 1.20, 1.40 and 1.80, rising steadily to the hotel. A cheap city's rent ladder is gentler, and its strongest step is the last.

What does that suggest?

That you should aim at three houses on every city of a country before spending on a fourth or a hotel. The extra rent per coin is highest there. The building rules help: by default houses must go up evenly across a country, so you cannot put three on one city and none on the others anyway.

It also suggests an order of spending. If you have cash for four houses, spread them so that each city gets to two before any gets three, then add the third where you can.

Why is the ladder shaped like this?

It reflects a common design for rent tables: a modest rise for the first houses and a steep one at three, followed by smaller steps for the last two. It rewards a player who commits to a set instead of dabbling.

What about the hotel?

A hotel costs the same as the fifth house and adds the same rent as the fourth. It is a solid purchase but not a leap. At the dear end of the board it also costs the most, 200 per step, so a full set of hotels in Canada costs 2,000.

How do you use it?

  • Plan for three houses on each city of a set first.
  • Keep a cash reserve of at least the cost of the houses you need.
  • Delay hotels until you have cash to spare.
  • On the cheap end, the hotel is the strongest step, since the earlier ones barely register.

Where can you see it?

Look at the rent table on a city in a game at Board It, a free place to play a property trading game online with friends. Work out the extra rent per coin yourself. It takes a minute and changes how you build.

One more reason to build early

Because the return at three houses is so large, a player who gets there first can win the game with one good landing.
