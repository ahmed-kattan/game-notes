Trivia questions for adults: ten history questions from Attila to the Bismarck

Ahmed Moaz · 1 November 2026

Trivia questions for adults can drop the childhood classics and ask for the thing behind the headline. This is a history round of ten, from the fall of Rome's world to the Second World War, pitched at people who remember some school history and want more. They are in rough order of difficulty. The last one asks for a year.

From Rome's last years to Napoleon

  1. On which South Atlantic island did Napoleon live out his last exile?
  2. Which Roman emperor joined in the decree of 313 that made Christianity legal in the empire?
  3. Which fifth-century leader of the Huns was nicknamed the Scourge of God?

Cairo, Mexico and Bletchley Park

  1. Which Egyptian president built the Aswan High Dam and took over the Suez Canal in 1956?
  2. Which Spanish conquistador landed in Mexico in 1519 and brought down the Aztec Empire?
  3. Which early electronic computer was built at Bletchley Park to read German codes?

The Second World War

  1. What was the code name for Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941?
  2. Which battle in December 1944 was Germany's last big attack in the west?
  3. Which German battleship was sunk in 1941 after a long chase through the Atlantic?
  4. Closest year: in which year did Constantinople fall to the Ottomans?

The answers

  1. Saint Helena. Napoleon had escaped an earlier exile, on Elba, before Waterloo.
  2. Constantine. The Edict of Milan, in 313, was issued with his co-emperor Licinius.
  3. Attila, who died in 453.
  4. Gamal Abdel Nasser. The Suez Crisis of 1956 followed.
  5. Hernan Cortes, who is said to have arrived with about 500 men.
  6. Colossus. The machine was kept secret until the 1970s.
  7. Operation Barbarossa, named after a medieval German emperor.
  8. The Battle of the Bulge, fought in the snowy Ardennes forest.
  9. The Bismarck, days after it had sunk HMS Hood.
  10. 1453. Some historians use that year to mark the end of the Middle Ages.

What makes history trivia work for grown-ups?

Adults tire of "who was the first president of" questions. They respond to a question with a small puzzle in it. Question 4 gives two clues, a dam and a canal. Question 9 gives a year and a chase. A player who does not know the answer can still work towards it, and that is what keeps the whole table involved.

I avoid pure date questions in the middle of a round. A date is either known or not, and it splits the table into two groups. I save one for the end, where a close guess earns something.

How hard should an adult history round be?

I would aim for about half the table getting the first three, a few getting questions 4 to 8, and one or two getting 9. Question 10 is for guessing. A year in the Middle Ages is not something most people know, but most can place it, and the nearest guess wins.

If your table is mostly history readers, swap in harder ones. If it is mixed, keep the mix as above and let the number question settle it.

Where does this round sit in a longer trivia quiz?

A history round suits second or third place, once people have warmed up and before they are tired. It sits well between a lighter round on food or music and a science round. Do not put it last, because a table that is tired of reading will skim the notes.

If the evening is a full trivia quiz of four or five rounds, I would give history the same number of questions as the others, so no one feels it was chosen to suit one person. Ten is a good length, since the first three can be read quickly and the last one gets the time it deserves.

Several questions here touch on war. Keep to the notes and the sources, and keep the host's tone level.

Handling disputes in history

History is where tables argue most, because many answers have a "traditionally" or a "usually" behind them. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 is a sound date. The end of the Middle Ages is not a date at all, and it appears in the notes only as something some historians say.

Write a note for each answer that separates what is firmly known from what is a convention. It takes a minute, and it stops the argument before it starts. Read it out after the answer, and the table gets a fact to take away.
