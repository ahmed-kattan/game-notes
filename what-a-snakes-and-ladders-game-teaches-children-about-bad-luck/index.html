What a snakes and ladders game teaches children about bad luck

Ahmed Moaz · 26 October 2026

Most parents choose a snakes and ladders game for its simple rules. Fewer notice what it teaches. In the first ten minutes a child learns that a long climb can be undone by one throw, that this happens to everyone, and that the game goes on. It is a small, safe lesson in bad luck, and the maths behind it is more reassuring than you might think.

How often does a child meet a snake?

Almost every game. On the board I run, 89.4% of games touch at least one snake. A game averages 4.23 snake landings and 3.32 ladder landings. So a child will meet a slide most times they play, and they will meet it more often than a ladder.

That is useful to know as a parent. If your six-year-old slides down a snake and bursts into tears, it is not because they are unlucky. It is because the board is built this way. Say so: "Nearly everybody lands on a snake in this game."

Why is it a safe place to lose?

The stakes are tiny, and the loss is shared. Every player slides. Nobody did anything wrong. There is no strategy to blame yourself for, so a child cannot conclude "I played badly". They can only conclude "the die was mean this time", which is a much healthier message.

The game also moves on quickly. A slide that feels like a disaster is followed by another throw, and sometimes by a ladder. Children see, over and over, that setbacks are followed by recoveries.

What can you say at the table?

A few phrases help:

  • "That one stung. Let's see what comes next."
  • "Everybody gets a snake sometimes."
  • "You are still in the game."
  • "Ladders are coming."

Avoid "never mind" or "it doesn't matter". It does matter to a child for a moment, and being heard makes it pass faster.

What does the old story add?

The game began in India as a lesson in virtues and vices, with ladders for good deeds and snakes for bad ones. In a modern family version, you can borrow the idea gently. After a game, ask: "What would you put on a ladder in your own life? What would be a snake?" Children often answer with ordinary things: helping a friend for a ladder, forgetting a coat for a snake.

How should adults behave?

Children copy the adults around them. If you groan at a snake, they learn to groan. If you laugh and shrug, they learn that too. Let a child win without throwing the game, since they can tell. Congratulate the winner warmly, whoever it is.

What about children who hate losing?

Some do, and no board game removes that. A few tricks:

  1. Play as a team, so a loss belongs to both.
  2. Make the first game a practice game with no winner.
  3. Praise how they handled a snake, not the result.
  4. End on a different, easy win, like a quick round of something else.

Can it be played at a distance?

Yes, and it is a good way for a grandparent to share the same small lessons from another city. A free table on Board It lets two players or up to eight play snakes and ladders online with friends and family, with the snakes and ladders rules as printed, so a child can lose a few snakes to a grandmother on a tablet and learn the same lesson.

The limit of the lesson

This is a small, gentle habit builder, not a cure for a hard day. It helps most when it is part of a regular family game night where losses are normal and winners change.
