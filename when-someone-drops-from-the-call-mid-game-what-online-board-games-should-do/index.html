When someone drops from the call mid-game: what online board games should do

Ahmed Moaz · 18 October 2026

It happens in every long evening: a router restarts, a phone battery dies or a child pulls out a cable. Someone drops from the call in the middle of a game. A good table is built for this. Here is what a well-made online game does, what you can do as the host and what to agree before the evening starts, with examples from the online board games on a free site I make, Boardit.

What should a table do when a player disconnects?

Three things. It should keep their seat, so they can return and carry on. It should keep the game moving, so nobody has to wait. And it should say clearly who is missing, so the rest of the table is not guessing. A table that does all three turns a disaster into a pause.

How long should the table wait?

A turn clock settles it. If a player is away when their turn comes, the clock runs out and the table plays an obvious move for them. That keeps the game going without anyone having to decide. Thirty seconds is a common length for the clock, and a returning player simply rejoins the game with the same seat.

What happens to their seat?

It stays theirs. The player rejoins by opening the same link, and the table gives their seat back. This is why you should keep the link in a place everyone can find it, such as the call chat. A player who loses the link loses the seat.

What can the host do?

  • Post the link again in the chat, so the returning player can find it quickly.
  • Ask the table to hold for a minute if the player is nearly back.
  • Let the clock run if the drop is long, and welcome them back at the next game.
  • Announce the plan, so nobody feels the game is being decided behind their back.

What should you agree before the evening?

Say it at the start: "If someone drops, we keep playing. They rejoin when they can." Most groups accept this at once, and it removes the awkward argument later. For a tournament or a game with money-free bragging rights at stake, agree the rule in writing.

What about the person who keeps dropping?

Their connection is the problem, not their commitment. Suggest a wired connection if they have one, or moving closer to the router. Offer shorter games, such as a quiz of about fourteen minutes or a snakes race, which suffer less from interruptions.

How do dice games and quizzes differ?

A dice race like Ludo recovers easily because each turn is separate. A quiz moves in rounds, so a missed question simply scores nothing. A drawing game loses the drawer's turn if they are away, but moves on to the next drawer. None of them stops dead.

Does the type of game matter for the host's plan?

Yes. Before you pick the evening's games, think about how each one copes with an empty seat. A short quiz or a snakes race recovers in a minute. A longer property game needs a clearer agreement, because one missing player holds a large share of the board. If your group has a shaky connection among its members, start with the short games and move to the long ones once everyone is settled.

Should you tell the group about the drop?

Say it plainly and kindly: "Sam's connection dropped, the clock will play for them." A calm sentence stops speculation and keeps the mood. Do not joke at the expense of the person who dropped, because they will read the chat when they return.

Treat a drop kindly

A drop is a hiccup, not a verdict. The group that treats it kindly keeps the evening alive.
