Why forced fun at work fails, and how to design optional social time for a distributed team

Ahmed Moaz · 17 October 2026

Most remote team socials are optional on paper. Everyone can tell which ones are not. The calendar invite comes from the manager, the camera is expected on, and the absence of the people who skipped gets noticed. Forced fun at work fails for a reason researchers have measured: the same game can lift people's mood or sink it, depending on whether they agreed to play. For a distributed team, designing for that agreement matters more than picking the right game.

What the "mandatory fun" study found

In a working paper revised in 2014, Ethan Mollick and Nancy Rothbard of the Wharton School asked whether games imposed at work deliver the benefits earlier research promised. They ran a field experiment with more than 200 salespeople, some of whom were given a game with a leaderboard.

The result split on one variable, employee consent. Where employees accepted the game, their positive feelings at work went up. Where they did not, those feelings went down. A follow-up lab study pointed to two sources of that consent: whether the game felt legitimate, and whether people felt they had some choice.

This was a sales game, not a Friday social, and one company is not every team. But the mechanism carries over.

A game is not fun in itself. It is fun when the people playing it chose to be there. So should team socials be optional? This study suggests yes, and that announcing it is not enough.

How optional turns into forced fun at work

An event becomes mandatory long before anyone says so. Four signals do most of the damage.

  • The manager is the host. Even a genuine "join if you like" from the person who writes your review reads as an instruction.
  • Attendance is visible. A list of names, a screenshot of the gallery view, a "missed you yesterday" message: each tells people that someone was counting.
  • It overruns. A thirty-minute slot that runs to fifty turns into an obligation, because leaving now looks rude.
  • The hour suits half the team. On a distributed team, a 4 pm slot in London is late morning in New York and evening in Delhi.

I would put it in a sentence: people can tell when fun is compulsory.

How to make a team social genuinely optional

Opt-in has to be built into the event, not announced at the top of it.

  1. Say it is optional, out loud, and make it true. A manager who sometimes skips the social makes skipping safe for everyone else.
  2. Count nothing. No attendance tracking, no list of names, no follow-up, no mention in a one-to-one. Pick tools that have nothing to count either. On Boardit, a guest gives only a name, and a guest who stops playing is deleted after 24 hours.
  3. Let people leave without a scene. Since 3 October 2026, pressing Leave at a Boardit table frees the seat and passes on the host role in all six games. Before that, three of the games kept your seat after you left, so the table carried on with an absent player in it.
  4. End on time. Book a short slot and finish inside it, even mid-game. A social that always ends when promised is one people can risk joining.
  5. Rotate the hour, or run it twice. If one time zone always gets the late slot, the event is optional for some and inconvenient for others.

All six games on Boardit, the free game site I build, run in a browser with nothing to install.

Choice helps too. The Wharton lab study tied consent to a sense of agency. Letting the group pick between two short online party games, or between a game and just talking, gives people a decision before the first round.

The case for some structure

Opt-in has a cost, and it falls on the people who most need the social. New starters and quieter colleagues are the least likely to join something optional, and on a remote team they have no corridor to fall back on.

So optional should not mean hidden. A short, regular slot that people know exists, with no penalty for missing it, is different from a once-a-quarter event with an attendance sheet. The first invites people in. The second checks whether they came.

For onboarding, a structured welcome with a named buddy is often better than a game. That is a different job, and a game is a poor substitute for it.

Where my argument runs out

The consent study is one working paper about a sales game at one company, not a social game, and I have read its abstract and coverage rather than the full paper. I also build one of the tools mentioned here, so I have an interest in teams playing multiplayer browser games at all. I have no data on how teams use it, and none on whether optional socials keep people longer. What the evidence supports is narrower: the same game feels different when people chose it.
